Introduction
Harry Potter was a 
wizard – a wizard fresh 
from his first year at 
Hogwarts School of Witchcraft 
and Wizardry. And if the 
Dursleys were unhappy to 
have him back for the 
holidays, it was nothing to 
how Harry felt. 
He missed Hogwarts so much 
it was like having a constant 
stomachache. He missed the 
castle, with its secret 
passageways and ghosts, his 
classes (though perhaps not 
Snape, the Potions master), 
the mail arriving by owl, eating 
banquets in the Great Hall, 
sleeping in his four-poster bed 
in the tower dormitory, visiting 
the gamekeeper, Hagrid, in his 
cabin next to the Forbidden 
Forest in the grounds, and, 
especially, Quidditch, the most 
popular sport in the wizarding 
world (six tall goal posts, four 
flying balls, and fourteen 
players on broomsticks). 
All Harry's spellbooks, his 
wand, robes, cauldron, and 
top–of–the–line Nimbus Two 
Thousand broomstick had 
been locked in a cupboard 
under the stairs by Uncle 
Vernon the instant Harry had 
come home. What did the 
Dursleys care if Harry lost his 
place on the House Quidditch 
team because he hadn't 
practiced all summer? What 
was it to the Dursleys if Harry 
went back to school without 
any of his homework done? 
The Dursleys were what 
wizards called Muggles (not a 
drop of magical blood in their 
veins), and as far as they were 
concerned, having a wizard in 
the family was a matter of 
deepest shame. Uncle Vernon 
had even padlocked Harry's 
owl, Hedwig, inside her cage, 
to stop her from carrying 
messages to anyone in the 
wizarding world. 
Harry Potter and the 
Chamber of Secrets.
This is the second edition 
of the (completely 
unofficial) Harry Potter 
Roleplaying Game. I owe a 
debt of gratitude to the Harry 
Potter Wiki, the official 
Pottermore website (as was), 
all those game creators that 
have come before me, and the 
many players who have 
contacted me with feedback. 
I’d like to particularly thank 
Constantin Carpenter, the 
Russian translator of the game, 
who’s additions have 
prompted me to fill some of 
the glaring omissions in the 
first edition of the HPRPG so 
that it continues to be the 
most complete Harry Potter 
roleplaying game. 
Additions to the second 
edition include expanded 
character creation with part–
breeds and rare traits, two new 
skills (Swimming and 
Persuasion), more detail on 
wand creation including the 
properties of different wand 
cores and how they interact 
with particular wand woods, 
rules for resisting magic and 
summoning your Patronus, 
new rules for enchanted 
objects and how to craft them, 
useful information about 
Herbology and Magizoology, 
stats for more magical 
creatures, animal companions 
like Hedwig and Scabbers, 
and some of the disreputable 
denizens of Knockturn Alley, 
and probably the most 
requested (and egregious) 
missing element, the rules 
for Quidditch. 
If that wasn’t enough, there is 
also new material about the 
Wizarding British Isles with 
detailed maps and information 
about Diagon Alley (including 
a price list), Knockturn Alley, 
Hogsmeade and, of course, 
Hogwarts School of Witchcraft 
and Wizardry.

How to Play
What is a 
Roleplaying Game? 
J
.K.Rowling’s wizarding 
world is an eccentric 
multi–layered realm of 
believable characters with 
unbelievable abilities. 
The ideal environment 
for a roleplaying game. 
Roleplaying games (RPGs) 
are a collaborative form of 
storytelling in which players 
assume the roles of characters 
in a fictional world. This game 
is set in the wizarding world of 
Harry Potter, so you might play 
students at Hogwarts, witches 
and wizards battling dark 
magic, or workaday wizards 
climbing the ladder in their 
magical careers. You will solve 
mysteries, witness wonders, 
and face terrible dangers with 
only your wits, courage, and 
magical training to keep 
you safe. 
A roleplaying game has rules 
like any other game, but they 
are designed to be as flexible 
as possible. Instead of telling 
you how to play, they provide a 
relatively straightforward 
framework for simulating the 
mechanics of the world, 
defining your character’s 
abilities and resolving 
dramatic situations. 
What you’ll need 
to start play... 
Keeping track of your 
character and their 
travels is vital, so pens 
and paper are essential. 
As well as jotting down notes 
and drawing maps, you’ll also 
need a record of your 
character’s abilities and 
current condition. These rules 
include a character sheet and 
spell sheet designed to help 
you create and track your 
character. You’ll also need: 
A Narrator 
The Narrator devises the story 
and describes the situations 
that the players encounter, as 
well as portraying all the 
colourful characters they meet 
on their way. The Narrator’s 
job is to keep the story flowing 
while keeping it flexible 
enough to accommodate the 
players’ inventiveness. 
The Narrator will need a good 
grasp of the rules as part of 
their job is to interpret them 
in a way that is fair to the 
players and doesn’t get in the 
way of the drama. They will 
also need a reasonable 
understanding of the Harry 
Potter universe. 
Players 
Ideally two or more people 
who will play the heroes of the 
Narrator’s story. Players don’t 
necessarily need an in–depth 
knowledge of the wizarding 
world. After all, Harry Potter 
himself entered it knowing 
nothing about its lore or 
workings. 
How dice are used 
In this game... 
Rolling dice is the 
primary mechanic for 
resolving actions and 
ensuring an element of 
uncertainty and risk in the 
players’ adventures. 
The Harry Potter RPG is 
played using ten–sided dice 
(abbreviated to D10 or D). 
These can be used in several 
ways. One way is to roll one or 
more dice and add any 
bonuses that might apply. 
For example, if you were asked 
to roll 3D10+2 you would roll 
three ten–sided dice and add 
two to the total rolled, or if 
asked to roll 2D+5 you would 
roll two ten–sided dice and 
add five to the result. 
You can also generate a 
number between 1 and 5 (1D5) 
by halving the roll of a single 
ten–sided die or between one 
and one–hundred (1D100) by 
rolling two dice and 
designating one as the tens 
and one as the ones or units. 
For example, rolling a three 
on the tens dice and a five on 
the ones dice gives you 35, 
while rolling a ten on both 
gives you a result of 100.

Success & Failure
Standard Test 
During the game you’ll come 
up against challenges that will 
test your abilities. and there 
are a number of mechanics to 
decide success or failure. 
Your characters abilities are 
defined by their Attributes 
(see page 12) and their Skills 
(pages 13). 
When you roll to attempt a 
task the standard mechanic is 
to roll a ten–sided die (1D) 
and add a relevant Attribute 
and Skill. 
There are some examples of 
Attribute/Skill pairings below. 
To succeed you will need to 
roll equal to or higher than a 
difficulty level chosen by the 
Narrator. 
The Narrator can also ask you 
to roll 1D plus two Attributes 
or 1D plus two skills if they 
think that better reflects the 
situation. 
Difficulty Level 
To succeed you need to roll 
equal to or higher than the 
difficulty levels chosen by the 
Narrator: 
Targets range from elementary 
tasks that you should normally 
be able to do without any 
difficulty (3+) to almost 
impossible tasks that only the 
most powerful witch or wizard 
will be able to achieve without 
help (25+). 
Levels of Success 
Sometimes it’s useful to know 
the level of success or failure. 
The table below allows you to

free to interpret what x2 and 
x3 results mean in tangible 
and dramatic terms. Some 
rules use specifically designed 
tables. For example, see the 
table for resisting damage on 
page 35 and the table for spell 
effects on page 41.

Quick Test 
Sometimes you’ll be asked to 
quickly test one of your 
Attributes or Skills. Roll 1D10. 
If you roll equal or less than 
the Attribute or Skill that 
you’re testing then you 
succeed. The narrator may 
award one or more points to 
add to the Attribute/Skill to 
reflect factors in your favour. 
Opposed Test 
Sometimes you’ll face 
opposition from another 
character. Both characters roll 
1D10 and add a relevant 
Attribute and Skill. For 
example, you’re sneaking past 
a Death Eater. It’s your Speed 
and Stealth versus their Wits 
and Observation. Whoever 
rolls highest wins. Ties are 
decided by the Narrator. 
Working Together 
Sometimes you need to 
combine your efforts to 
succeed. The Narrator decides 
how many people can 
realistically work together. 
Poring over a document may 
only be practical for two or 
three to do, whereas searching 
a room could be possible with 
any number of people. One 
person oversees the activity 
and they roll as normal, but 
gain a bonus equal to +1 for 
each person helping. For 
example, you roll 1D10 and 
add you Wits and Observation 
to search for clues. With three 
people aiding your search you 
add +3 to your roll. 
Story Points 
Story Points reflect important 
experiences in the character’s 
life and allow the player to 
expand their character’s 
abilities in response to those 
experiences. They can also be 
used to help a character 
succeed at a task as a 
reflection of the character’s 
growing confidence. 
Your character can earn a 
Story Point by playing through 
some of the following life 
events: 
A significant first 
Your first class at Hogwarts, 
your first Charms class, your 
first duel, your first Quidditch 
match, your first love. All these 
things can earn you a point. 
A personal victory 
Ron and Harry would 
certainly have earned a Story 
Point for defeating a Mountain 
Troll in their first year, and 
Hermione would have earned 
a point for creating the 
complex Polyjuice Potion in 
her second year. Player’s 
should earn a Story Point for 
completing an adventure. For 
particularly long scenarios the 
Narrator may want to split the 
story into chapters and reward 
one or more Story Points for 
each chapter completed. 
Being amazing 
Any time you do anything 
outstandingly brave, or 
outlandishly clever, or are in 
any way dashed impressive, 
you deserve a Story Point. 
Using Story Points 
You can use Story Points to 
grasp victory from failure or 
even succeed at tasks that 
would normally be beyond 
your abilities. 
If you make a roll and fail to 
meet the target you can spend 
a Story Point to roll an 
additional D10 and add it to 
your roll. If your roll with the 
additional dice added is still 
too low to meet the target then 
you still fail. You cannot 
continue to spend Story 
Points to roll additional dice. 
Sometimes you are just fated 
to fail. 
Although there is still a 
chance of failure with 
standard and opposed tests, 
quick tests are handled more 
generously. You can use a 
Story Point to automatically 
succeed at a Quick Test 
without having to roll any 
dice. 
You can also use Story Points
to permanently increase
Attributes and Skills.

Creating a Character
Outline Your Character. 
Your name, your lineage, and your 
school (normally Hogwarts), year, 
and House. If your character has 
left school, you can also choose a 
profession (see pages 19–20). 
Draw a portrait of your character.
Define Your Attributes. Your 
ability in important areas: Vitality, 
Speed, Wits, Knowledge, and 
Focus. See page 12 for details. 
Roll (or choose) the components 
of your wand (see page 24) and 
choose a pet (page 12).
Choose Yours Skills. Distribute 
points based on your education 
and interests (see page 13). You 
can also add professional skills if 
your school days are behind you 
(see pages 19–20). There is a full 
list of skills on pages 21–23.
This is where you’ll record any 
injuries or afflictions you pick up 
on your adventures. The 
wizarding world is a dangerous 
place, but you don’t need to worry 
about that just yet...
The Harry Potter Roleplaying Game gives 
you all the tools you need to play 
characters in the world of Harry Potter. 
You can play fellow Gryffindor students 
attending Hogwarts at the same time as Harry, 
Ron, and Hermione. Play students in a different 
house, a different school, or even a different 
time. You can even bypass Hogwarts altogether 
and jump straight into a Wizarding career. Are 
you a buttoned down Ministry of Magic Officer, 
or a rakish pureblood dilettante? A dashing 
Magizoologist working in the field, or a dusty 
Hogwarts Professor? You’ll certainly have your 
own ideas, and it’s a good idea to share them 
with the Narrator and the other players. 
It’s important to build a group of interlocking 
characters that will fit naturally into the stories 
that the Narrator plans to tell. 
Your character’s personal details, abilities, and 
knowledge are recorded on a character sheet. 
The sheet helps you to keep track of your 
character’s progress as well as their current 
physical and mental state.
